\chapter{Design Games}
\cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006} describes Design games as a way of organizing participation 
in design projects. \cite{triantafyllakosDesigningEducationalSoftware2011} notes that they (Design Games) 
provide a starting point for facilitating user participation and contribution.

    \section{Mechanics of Design Games}
    Throughout the literature review a number of reoccuring mechanics have been identified. 
    The mechanics are subsequently clustered in 4 Clusters of effects they have on co-design sessions 
    using design games. Many mechanics can be attributed to multiple effects and are 
    therefore included in more than one cluster. Mechanics are ordered alphabetically. 

        \subsection{Break existing habits}
        Breaking existing habits is a shift from everday logical thinking to generative, 
        imaginative and lateral thinking. It grounds abstract thinking into a concrete 
        reality so participants aren't overwhelmed by a blank canvas and overcomes potential 
        power relations participants are part of usually. 

            \subsubsection{Chance}
            Element of chance stems from the surrealist movement. "Games are often based on a wish 
            to get out of habits, experience new and be taken by surprise" \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign}. 
            This might come in the form of a dice, used to decide which topic or question to work on 
            next. 

            \subsubsection{Combining elements that do not fit together}
            People can be triggered out of their normal thinking patterns by, sometimes unusual, 
            associations, suggestions or actions writen on a card \cite{royCardbasedDesignTools2019}

            \subsubsection{Element of fun \& Ludic drive}
            The essence of play lies in the intensity and absorption of players. It is referred to as the 
            "Fun element". This absorption has to be based on voluntary participation and acceptance of the 
            game rules \cite{brandtFormattingDesignDialogues2008}. 

            \subsubsection{Facilitation}
            A facilitator can leave out elements to increase the focus more on a specific topic or influence 
            which topic or question is to be worked on next \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}

            \subsubsection{Game rules}
            When playing, participants impersonate new roles that are defined by rules. Rules define what goal players strive to achieve, 
            how success is defined and what actions are allowed. Thus, rules establish creative freedom by excluding 
            ordinary life, while simultaneously forming its boundaries \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            A defined winning condition and rules to guide argumentation are found to make it easier for participants 
            to reach consensus, which is described as more troublesome in classic co-design workshops with strangers \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Involving stakeholders}
            Participants should come from different backgrounds and explore cooperation through playing. 
            Design Games establish out-of-the-ordinary communication that breaks existing routines and 
            communication habits, allowing new problem solving approaches to emerge \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}

            \subsubsection{Magic circle}
            The magic circle is a temporary sphere of activity in which the laws of ordinary life do not count. 
            It invites participants to think beyond the ordinary and envision alternative solutions \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}

            \subsubsection{Presupplied input}
            How topics are framed by the supplied game material matters to the outcome of the game \cite{brandtFormattingDesignDialogues2008}. 

            \subsubsection{Tangible game material}
            Through Concept Design Games it has been found that Game Pieces and Game Boards can be abstracted to 
            eliminate functional knowledge and former experiences participants bring with them \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}
        


        \subsection{Come up with creative ideas \& Overcoming cognitive intertia}
        Autors talk about "scaffolding" to help non-designers access their creativity. Without some 
        structure, people default to what they already know, or experience analysis paralysis. 

            \subsubsection{Abstracted and ambiguous tasks}
            Help participants to propose new interpretations and alternative solutions to come up with 
            creative ideas \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Boundary objects}
            Boundary objects make arguments tangible and help participants to focus \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 
            
            \subsubsection{Chance}
            Elements of chance, for example through dice throwing, decide for the player which questions 
            are to be worked on next \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}. Another method is 
            randomly drawing cards, which then provide a starting point for discussion or building a scenario \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 
            Games often remain open-ended until the very end, allowing for new twists through elements of 
            chance and luck \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Combining elements that do not fit together}
            Steming from the surrealist movement, some creativity techniques are based on combining elements which 
            normally do not fit together \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 
            Cards allow to easily violate cateogries of information by physically placing them different, which 
            allows the brains creativity to come in and repair them in an ad-hoc manner \cite{royCardbasedDesignTools2019}. 
            
            \subsubsection{Design Context}
            Design Games are specifically designed for a specific context in mind. Depending on this context, 
            different game qualitites are desirable, and different participants are needed \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, 
            \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}, \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Establishing an informal atmosphere}
            Design Games are usually played in an informal atmosphere, which is shown to be the most productive 
            in creative work \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 
            
            \subsubsection{Facilitation}
            A facilitator can lead the workshop and present the agenda, game pieces used and rules to follow. 
            Facilitators can also take an active role and drive the process forward by making suggestions, as well as 
            encouraging players to make moves \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 
            Further, dividing the design session into sequential phases has shown to drive the process forward in a more 
            natural way, which was found similar to divergent and convergent design thinking \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Game rules}
            Rules set the boundaries for whats possible and also structure the play of the game. The shift from the goals 
            of the design process to the rules of a game makes it easier to generate design moves. Further, restrictions on 
            idea generation is shown to improve creative outcome \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 

            \subsubsection{Open-endedness}
            Open-ended and ambiguous tasks help participants to propose new interpretations and alternative solutions \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            Game material, e.g. cards, can supply dedicaded space where participants can add their own notes \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}, \cite{nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}. 
            Lastly, design games themselves are open-ended and open for evolution \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}

            \subsubsection{Presupplied input}
            A question, e.g. from cards, might be used to spark further discussion \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, 
            \cite{nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}. 
            Predesigned material can also help in setting the frame and provide starting point for the design 
            collaboration \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}. 
            Graphically represented themes on, e.g. cards, can guide conversations throughout the session \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}. 
            Cards can further be used to summarize good design practises or domain knowledge and hint participants towards 
            it during the design session \cite{royCardbasedDesignTools2019}, \cite{nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}

            \subsubsection{Scenarios}
            Design Games construct scenarios that are both concrete and flexible. They represent a particular interpretation 
            of a design situation. \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. Scenario construction can be used 
            for creating and testing design ideas with future users. Generic game pieces can be provided for that or users 
            encouraged to produce their own \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 

            \subsubsection{Split participants in groups}
            Participants may play the same game in parallel, split in groups. Thus each group comes to their own conclusion \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            Another approach is to let participants first individually create ideas to then share them with the rest of their team or 
            between groups \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Tangible game material}
            Visual and tangible components in design games are generative tools. They aim to create a platform for 
            shared focus of attention and also provide documentation and reminder during and after the co-design 
            session \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            Ease of moving around cards on the table and pairing them new enabled creation of links across themes 
            and gain new perspectives into underlying causes of some challenges \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}. 
            
            \subsubsection{Time keeping}
            Keeping track of time can improve structure of discussion and prompt the team to conclude their discussions \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            Time keeping also aids in equally distributing time between the different topics to discuss \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            In design games players often do not compete against each other, but might collaborate to "race against the clock". \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Win condition}
            There is rarely competition against each other in design game. However, tension is a quality of design games and 
            aids the creative process \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            Misalignment between goals - for example an 
            individual one for the player and an overall one for the game - can also impede the success of the design game 
            \cite{kessnerWickedPlayWicked2020}, \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

        \subsection{Create common understanding}
        Multiple participants from different backgrounds participate in co-design sessions. Design Games often 
        allow a common language to to emerge between players. Constraints imposed by the game force participants 
        to translate their tacit knowledge into this shared language and be explicit in their moves and explanations. 

            \subsubsection{Abstracted and stylized game pieces}
            Abstracted and stylized game pieces prompt participants to be explicit about their understanding and views 
            \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}. 

            \subsubsection{Boundary objects}
            Boundary objects make arguments and discussions tangible. They create a comon ground everybody can relate to. 
            They are rich enough to span the gap between different understandings \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 

            \subsubsection{Game rules}
            Rules help bridge the gap between different languages of participants. They create a common ground everyone 
            can relate to \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}
            Vague rules trigger players to be explicit in describing how they understand and interpret them. \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            Rules can change established communication patterns among participants. They prompt players to voluntarily 
            overcome unnecessary obstacles, for example by prescribing when players whould explicitly discuss a certain theme 
            with a stakeholder. This creates a shift to more explicit communication patterns \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Presupplied Input}
            Sometimes proposals or details about designs are supplied preprinted to the design-game session. Participants 
            can point towards certain details in the proposal during the decision making arguments visible for everyone \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}.

            \subsubsection{Tangible game material}
            Being tangible, game material helps participants to keep track of their functions and role within negotiations \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 
            They create a common ground every participant can relate to. While allowing different participants to read and interpret the material 
            differently, they are rich enough in content to bridge the gap between different understandings \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 
            Tangible game material further allows every player to see the current state of information \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}, 
            \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}. 
            By moving pieces across a game board the discussion is captured physically for every participant to see \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 

        \subsection{Learn about concepts participants hold \& Build empathy}
        Design games build bridges to the participants everyday lives and allow conclusions to be drawn about them. \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}
        Through playing in different roles players develop empathy for different perspectives. Abstracted game pieces and ambiguous tasks 
        prompt players to make explicit the concepts and knowledge they hold. 

            \subsubsection{Abstracted and stylized game pieces}
            Abstracted and stylized game pieces prompt participants to be explicit about their understanding and views. This allows 
            to learn about concepts and knowledge participants hold \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, 
            \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}

            \subsubsection{Acting}
            Acting, like stage performers, helps to gain empathy for the experiences of the persons to design for. It also allows participants to 
            switch roles and gain new perspectives on a topic. \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}, \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            Letting participants enact scenarios further makes visible concept participants hold, through the approaches and choices of scenes
            they choose to enact \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}. 

            \subsubsection{Boundary objects}
            Tangible boundary objects make visible how other participants interpret the material. \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}

            \subsubsection{Chance}
            Elements of chance, for example a dice throw, can be used to, for example, select a role through their perspective participants 
            should discuss a story. This allows participants to empathize with this role. \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}

            \subsubsection{Magic if}
            When acting scenarios, an actress has to ask herself like "if the character was in this situation - how would she react?" 
            \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}

            \subsubsection{Participant concepts}
            Design games do not simply generate data or facilitate collaboration but also serve as tool for exploring and challenging 
            perspectives of both researchers and participants. For example, family consultants confronted with stories created in an earlier 
            game round were surprisingly unfamiliar and different from what the researchers expected \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}. 

            \subsubsection{Roles}
            Design games often contain role-play activity. In performances, play and games the reactions of performers and 
            audience are real, even though the actions that trigger these are fictional. Which allows to learn about the concepts 
            participants hold \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 

            \subsubsection{Rules}
            Participants interpret rules against the background of their own experiences and expectations, allowing those to become 
            explicit \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Tangible game material}
            Tangible game pieces allow participants to use visual, auditory and tactile senses, which evoke more reflections and 
            comments than otherwise \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. Tactile pieces also allow participants to discuss 
            concepts through the eyes of different stakeholders \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}. 


        \subsection{Overcome power relations \& Lower communication barriers in a group setting}
        Participatory design aims to include affected stakeholders in the design process and thus equalize power relations. Various 
        game mechanics used in design games support that. Collaboratively working to win against a shared obstacle, rules structuring 
        communication structure, turns to equalize attention among players and an informal atmosphere help to equalize power hierarchies 
        and lower communication barriers. 

            \subsubsection{Acting}
            Having users create and explore scenarios in their own environment puts them on more equal footing to 
            the design team \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 

            \subsubsection{Agenda \& Facilitation}
            Often game designers, researchers or a facilitor designs beforehand what issues to include in the session. 
            This can negatively impact or manifest power relations in a hidden way \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}

            \subsubsection{Chance}
            Elements of chance, e.g. a dice or some wheel, can be used to decide which participant to start with their turn \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}.

            \subsubsection{Competition}
            Design games usually do not include competition among participants but are often designed such that participants need 
            to collaborate to overcome a shared obstacle, levelling power relations \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}.

            \subsubsection{Fun}
            The game like format showed to lower the entry barrier to join discussions \cite{nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}. 
            Immersing stakeholders in the mindset of play appears to blend and balance their usual power relations between each other. 
            A drive behind this might be the ludic drive \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}, \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Involve stakeholders}
            Participatory design tools, like design games, encourage stakeholders and users to participate, which encourages 
            cross-disciplinary collaboration \cite{nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}, \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. 
            Often people with different backgrounds, expertise and interests are thus included and their perspectives and interests 
            added to the discussion \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Open-endedness}
            Open-ended assignments allow participants to interpret and influence n what to focus on in the design \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}.

            \subsubsection{Rules}
            Playing by the rules plays down power relations between participants \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}, \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 
            They can change the communication patterns as they prompt players to voluntarily overcome obstacles, for example, by prescribing 
            when players should explicitly discuss a certain topic with stakeholders \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Time keeping}
            Enforcing time rules allows to equally distribute time between players taking turns or between issues to discuss \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            Also time can be used as an obstacle players have to collaboratively win against when playing the game \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}.

            \subsubsection{Turn taking}
            Turn taking divides attention among players and allows participation on more equal terms \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, 
            \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}, \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}.
