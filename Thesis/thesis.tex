        \section{Mechanics of Design Games}
    Design games are most of the time purpose-built for a specific design context \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 
    Various frameworks have been proposed in the co-design literature to provide building blocks for the development 
    of design games \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006,vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
    Nonetheless, through the literature review done in this thesis, a set of 22 recurring mechanics have been identified.  

    For further analysis, these mechanics were grouped into five clusters according to the effects they have 
    when employed in a co-design workshop. A single mechanic can be attributed to multiple effects, depending on 
    the lens it is viewed through. Those mechanics are grouped into more than one cluster of effects. For each mechanic 
    a brief explanation grounded in the literature describes its connection with the effect it is attributed for.

        \subsection{Break existing habits}
        Breaking existing habits is a shift from everyday logical thinking to generative, 
        imaginative and lateral thinking. It grounds abstract thinking into a concrete 
        reality so participants aren't overwhelmed by a blank canvas and overcomes potential 
        power relations participants are usually part of. 

            \subsubsection{Chance}
            A dice or randomly drawing cards might be used to select which topics or questions to discuss, 
            easing the pressure to decide where to start and avoiding participants selecting the topics 
            they are most comfortable with \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006, hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016,bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 
            
            \subsubsection{Combining elements that do not fit together}
            "(..) people can be triggered out of their normal thinking patterns by, sometimes unusual, 
            associations, suggestions or actions written and/on illustrated on cards" \cite{royCardbasedDesignTools2019}

            \subsubsection{Element of fun \& Ludic drive}
            Players are absorbed in play. This is commonly referred to as the "Fun element" \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 

            \subsubsection{Facilitation}
            A facilitator can leave out elements to increase the focus more on a specific topic or influence 
            which topic or question is to be worked on next \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}

            \subsubsection{Game rules}
            Design games establish a distinct space for creative exploration that is separate from everyday life. This space 
            is created by the game's rules, which define the objectives, criteria of success and allowed actions for the role 
            players adopt during game playing \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 
            Furthermore, rules help to establish structured discussions which is shown to make it easier for participants 
            to reach consensus \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Involving stakeholders}
            Design games involve stakeholders from different backgrounds and invite them to collaborate. By doing so 
            existing routines are halted and out of ordinary communication is established that allows new problem solving 
            approaches to emerge \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Magic circle}
            Magic circle is a sphere of experience distinct from ordinary life. It may be achieved, for 
            example, by a special meeting room setup. By entering the magic circle, participants think beyond their 
            usual patterns and are invited to invision alternative solutions \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014,fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}

            \subsubsection{Presupplied input}
            How topics are framed by the supplied game material matters to the outcome of the game \cite{brandtFormattingDesignDialogues2008}. 

            \subsubsection{Tangible game material}
            Through Concept Design Games it has been found that Game Pieces and Game Boards can be abstracted to 
            eliminate functional knowledge and former experiences participants bring with them \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}

        \subsection{Come up with creative ideas \& Overcoming cognitive intertia}
        Design games create a kind of "scaffolding" to help non-designers access their creativity. 
        Without some structure, people default to what they already know, or experience analysis paralysis. 

            \subsubsection{Abstracted and ambiguous tasks}
            Help participants to propose new interpretations and alternative solutions to come up with 
            creative ideas \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Boundary objects}
            Boundary objects make arguments tangible and help participants to focus \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 
            
            \subsubsection{Chance}
            Elements of chance can be used to decide for players which topic or questions to be worked on next, 
            avoiding analysis paralysis \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}. A specific example is to randomly 
            draw cards, which can then act as starting point for discussions or as creative input for building a scenario 
            \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. Design games are often created open-ended to allow pivots in decisions caused by 
            unforseen events up until the very end \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Combining elements that do not fit together}
            Combining elements that normally do not fit together is a technique that can be used to spark creativity \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 
            Another approach is to violate known information by, for example, placing cards wrongly. This causes the brain's 
            creativity to repair the violation in an ad-hoc manner \cite{royCardbasedDesignTools2019}. 
            
            \subsubsection{Design Context}
            Design Games are specifically designed for a specific context in mind. Depending on this context, 
            different game qualitites are desirable, and different participants are needed \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, 
            \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}, \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Establishing an informal atmosphere}
            "Game playing creates an informal atmosphere, which is the most productive in creative work" 
            \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 
            
            \subsubsection{Facilitation}
            A facilitator can lead the workshop and present the agenda, game pieces used and rules to follow \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            Facilitators can also take an active role during the workshop and drive the process forward by making suggestions, 
            as well as encouraging players to make moves, unblocking negotiations \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 

            \subsubsection{Game rules}
            Rules set the boundaries for whats possible and also structure the play of the game. The shift from the goals 
            of the design process to the rules of a game makes it easier to generate design moves. Further, restrictions on 
            idea generation is shown to improve creative outcome \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 

            \subsubsection{Open-endedness}
            Design games foster open-ended exploration by combining ambiguous prompts \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014} 
            and tactile artifacts—such as game cards—that invite participant annotation \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}, 
            allowing the structure of the game itself to evolve organically through play \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}.

            \subsubsection{Presupplied input}
            Presupplied materials as Input support the creative process in various ways. 
            Premade prompts can spark creativity and discussions \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006, nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}, 
            while predesigned items help in establishing the frame and context of the session \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014, gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}. 
            Throughout the workshop, visual themes help guide ongoing conversations \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}. 
            Additionally, cards can be used to hint participants towards best practices in design or 
            remind them of domain specifics \cite{royCardbasedDesignTools2019, nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}.
            
            \subsubsection{Scenarios}
            Design Games construct scenarios that are both concrete and flexible - "They represent a particular interpretation 
            of a design situation" \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. Scenario construction can be used 
            for creating and testing design ideas with future users. Generic game pieces can be provided for that or users 
            encouraged to produce their own \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 

            \subsubsection{Split participants in groups}
            Participants may play the same game in parallel, split in groups. Thus each group comes to their own conclusion \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            Another approach is to let participants first individually create ideas to then share them with the rest of their team or 
            between groups \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Tangible game material}
            In design games, visual and physical elements establish shared focus for participants 
            while acting as continuous reference material during and after the design session 
            \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            Furthermore, the ability to easily move and combine physical elements, like cards, helps participants 
            to more easily connect different themes, offering new insights \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}.
            
            \subsubsection{Time keeping}
            Keeping track of time can improve structure of discussion and prompt the team to conclude their discussions \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            Time keeping also aids in equally distributing time between the different topics to discuss \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            In design games players often do not compete against each other, but might collaborate to "race against the clock". \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Win condition}
            There is rarely competition against each other in design game. However, tension is a quality of design games and 
            aids the creative process \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            Misalignment between goals - for example an 
            individual one for the player and an overall one for the game - can also impede the success of a design game session
            \cite{kessnerWickedPlayWicked2020, fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

        \subsection{Create common understanding}
        Multiple participants from different backgrounds participate in co-design sessions. Design Games often 
        allow a common language to emerge between players. Constraints imposed by the game force participants 
        to translate their tacit knowledge into this shared language and be explicit in their moves and explanations. 

            \subsubsection{Abstracted and stylized game pieces}
            Abstracted and stylized game pieces prompt participants to be explicit about their understanding and views 
            \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}. 

            \subsubsection{Boundary objects}
            Boundary objects make discussions tangible. They externalize arguments and concepts so that a common ground is 
            established to which every participant can relate to. At the same time they are rich enough to close the gap 
            between different viewpoints \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 

            \subsubsection{Game rules}
            Rules serve as a bridge across disciplinary divides, establishing a common ground that all participants can understand 
            \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. Furthermore, rules dictate communication patterns, prompting direct dialogue 
            between players to help them navigate game obstacles \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. When these rules are 
            intentionally left vague, they encourage participants to openly articulate their own interpretations and perspectives 
            \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}.

            \subsubsection{Presupplied Input}
            Sometimes proposals or details about designs are supplied preprinted to the design-game session. Participants 
            can point towards certain details in the proposal during the decision making arguments visible for everyone \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}.

            \subsubsection{Tangible game material}
            Tangible game pieces capture discussions physically for every participant to see \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            They also help participants to track their role within negotiations. Similar to boundary objects, they are rich enough to allow participants to 
            interpret them differently, while at the same time bridging the gap between different disciplines and understandings \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}. 

        \subsection{Learn about concepts participants hold \& Build empathy}
        Through playing different roles players develop empathy for different perspectives. Abstracted game pieces and ambiguous tasks 
        prompt players to make explicit the concepts and knowledge they hold. 

            \subsubsection{Abstracted and stylized game pieces}
            Abstracted and stylized game pieces prompt participants to be explicit about their understanding and views. This allows 
            to learn about concepts and knowledge participants hold \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}, 
            \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}

            \subsubsection{Acting}
            Enacting scenarios helps participants to build empathy for the experiences of the persons to design for. It also allows participants to 
            switch roles and gain new perspectives on a topic. \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}, \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 
            The choice of scenes and what details to include further makes visible concepts participants hold \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}. 
            "In performances, play and games the reactions of performers and audience are real, even though the actions that trigger these are fictional" 
            \cite{vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014}. 

            \subsubsection{Boundary objects}
            Tangible boundary objects make visible how other participants interpret the material. \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}

            \subsubsection{Chance}
            Elements of chance, for example a dice throw, can be used to, for example, select a role through their perspective participants 
            should then discuss a story. This allows participants to empathize with this role. \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}

            \subsubsection{Game rules}
            Participants interpret rules against the background of their own experiences and expectations, allowing those to become 
            explicit \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Magic if}
            The magic if describes the question of "if this person is in this situation - how would she react?". When enacting scenarios, 
            actors frequently need to ask themselves this question which increases empathy for the role chosen to enact 
            \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 

            \subsubsection{Participant concepts}
            Design games do not simply generate designs or facilitate collaboration but are a research tool itself. They make explicit and 
            challenge perspectives of both participants and researchers. Reactions and outcome from involving stakeholders can be 
            surprisingly unfamiliar and different from what researchers initially expect \cite{kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021}. 

            \subsubsection{Tangible game material}
            Tangible game pieces allow participants to use more of their senses. They make concepts and thought processes explicit and 
            allow more reflection \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. Certain game pieces also allow participants to think through 
            the eyes of a different role \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}. 

        \subsection{Overcome power relations \& Lower communication barriers in a group setting}
        Participatory design aims to include affected stakeholders in the design process and thus equalize power relations. Various 
        game mechanics used in design games support that. Collaboratively working to win against a shared obstacle, rules structuring 
        communication structure, turns to equalize attention among players and an informal atmosphere help to equalize power hierarchies 
        and lower communication barriers. 

            \subsubsection{Acting}
            Scenarios can be explored by enacting them. Having users create and enact those scenarios, while within their own environment 
            lessens the power distance to the design team \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}.

            \subsubsection{Agenda \& Facilitation}
            Often game designers, researchers or a facilitor decides beforehand what issues to include in the session. 
            This can negatively impact or manifest power relations in a hidden way \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}

            \subsubsection{Chance}
            Elements of chance, e.g. a dice or some wheel, can be used to decide which participant to start with their turn \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}.

            \subsubsection{Competition}
            Design games usually do not include competition among participants but are often designed such that participants need 
            to collaborate to overcome a shared obstacle, levelling power relations \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}.

            \subsubsection{Fun}
            The game like format of design games seems to balance usual power relations between participants and stakeholders \cite{gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}, 
            \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. It also shows a lower entry barrier for participants to join discussions \cite{nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}. 

            \subsubsection{Game Rules}
            Adhering to game rules minimizes power imbalances between participants \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a}, \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. 
            Rules alter standard communication patterns by encouraging players to willingly navigate obstacles, such as following specific guidelines 
            on when to engage in explicit discussions with stakeholders \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Involve stakeholders}
            Participatory design tools, like design games, encourage stakeholders and users to participate, which encourages 
            cross-disciplinary collaboration \cite{nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}, \cite{zhangAddressingCulturalInertias2024}. 
            Often people with different backgrounds, expertise and interests are thus included and their perspectives and interests 
            added to the discussion \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 

            \subsubsection{Open-endedness}
            The open-endedness of assignments and tasks in design games allows participant to interpret them and thus exercise 
            influence on what to focus on in the design \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}.

            \subsubsection{Time keeping}
            Enforcing time rules allows to equally distribute time between players taking turns or between issues to discuss \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}. 
            Time can also be used as an obstacle players have to collaboratively win against when playing the game \cite{fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}.

            \subsubsection{Turn taking}
            Turn taking divides attention among players and allows participation on more equal terms \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, 
            \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}, \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016}.
            

        \subsection{Discussion}
        Table~\ref{tab:mechanics} shows that the mechanic \lstinline|Game rules| can be attributed to every effect cluster. 
        This hints at the essential role \lstinline|Game rules| play in design games. They structure the game play and define 
        what is possible while simultaneously lowering power differences \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a,vaajakallioDesignGamesCodesign2014,fikarGroundedTheoryZur2023}. 

        A central role can also be attributed to the mechanics \lstinline|Chance| and \lstinline|Tangible game material| which can be attributed 
        to four effects respectively. 
        \lstinline|Chance| can capture players by surprise and be attributed to the surrealist movement \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}, 
        but also plays a central role in equalizing power relations by taking away certain decisions, sparking creativity through random input 
        and break existing habits \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016,kjaersgaardDesignGamesFieldwork2021,bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 
        \lstinline|Tangible game material| are a central element to Games in general. Be it a game board, game pieces, cards, dice or props used 
        for enacting scenarios - Games use tangible game pieces. They fulfill multiple roles: On one hand physcial pieces allow everyone 
        to see the current state of game and discussion \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a,eriksenTakingDesignGames2014,gudiksenExplicatingStakeholderCriteria2017}. 
        Most of the time they are also abstracted and can be interpreted in multiple ways, while at the same time establishing common 
        ground between participants with different backgrounds \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006}. By doing so abstracted game 
        pieces function as \lstinline|Boundary objects| and are thus interconnected with the mechanics \lstinline|Abstraction| and \lstinline|Boundary objects|.
        Lastly, they also support creativity by allowing out-of-the-ordinary connections to be drawn by physically manipulating pieces or cards 
        \cite{hatamiFacilitatingServiceInteractions2016,nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}.

        \lstinline|Abstraction| and \lstinline|Boundary objects| are closely connected with \lstinline|Tangible game material|.  
        Lasty, we want to look at \lstinline|Facilitation| and \lstinline|Presupplied input| which are attributed to three effect clusters. 
        \lstinline|Facilitation| can help to break existing habits and spark creativity by unblocking discussions and setting up an agenda. 
        Additionally preparing the proper workspace, for example by ordering chairs like in a theatre stage, can help participants to move 
        from their ordinary life to a game mindset \cite{brandtFacilitatingCollaborationDesign2004a,bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. 
        \lstinline|Facilitation| can also hinder open-endedness of design game or enforce power relations when, for example certain topics are 
        preselected without input from participants \cite{eriksenTakingDesignGames2014}.
        \lstinline|Presupplied input| can help in multiple ways, it can spark creativity by introducting unforseen input or starting with 
        a specific question or topic \cite{brandtDesigningExploratoryDesign2006,bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024} - in that way it also breaks 
        existing habits \cite{bunaesRulesPlayBalance2024}. By setting the topic they create common ground and can also be used to hint or 
        remind participants of certain domain methods or design approaches \cite{royCardbasedDesignTools2019, nymarkernstsenTechnologyCardsDesign2023}.
