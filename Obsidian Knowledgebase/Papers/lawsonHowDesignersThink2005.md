fourth edition of Bryan Lawson’s book _How Designers Think: The Design Process Demystified_ (Architectural Press/Elsevier, 2005)

Summary of Chapter 16: "Towards a model of designing" (pp. 289–301)

In this final chapter (which was newly added for the 4th edition), Lawson synthesizes the observations from the rest of the book. Rather than proposing a rigid, step-by-step "route map" (which he criticizes earlier in the book), he offers a model of designing based on **cognitive skills and activities** that designers fluidly move between. 1. Rejection of Linear "Route Maps"

Lawson begins the chapter by reiterating that traditional, sequential models (such as moving strictly from _Analysis_ → _Synthesis_ → _Evaluation_) fail to capture how experienced designers actually work. In practice, design problems and solutions emerge together, and designers constantly shift focus rather than following a fixed chronological order. 2. The Core Design Skills / Activities

To replace linear steps, Lawson breaks the design process down into a set of distinct, overlapping cognitive activities (or "design skills"): [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESdAHEIkXXDvFihzvjGdzE4KTe69QCjBMOWe-CWrK4q2kFbCxkMaRkJCNVhudiNPIF6q-NXOtKD3Tb3ZlOwBwe7YOaemUUnsrZ66UAlr2Eg16WcAqe2XPvT0m5D60SvwI-hNzMJ9fN9R-6Hw8UVOoDwpzJtjif)]

- **Formulating:** The ability to understand, explore, and frame the design problem. This includes identifying constraints, recognizing what is missing from the brief, and deciding where to focus attention. [[1](https://books.google.at/books?id=0woDlFpL5xoC), [2](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESngEBxCJF129Z__P08bDrEeAnmE99vYIbKONIAcgNFnP8sgLgmWr128cQ2ctA6Hy8cVRAntV8JMeEfCkUoKoi1Vf-g4vY3UTlFaO9p3S7qT5RZqkUp-IwMA5J3hV8RZ_UhYM_LmPDHtg4LS9ezs8J3piAwhFX-xTWDIfGI9tFyW4vGq4GTkM_385QQCV5OaCynazjM_QqIl2YJ_uhNtgb9w)]

- **Representing:** Externalizing thoughts through drawings, sketches, physical models, computer models, or words. Lawson emphasizes that representations are not just ways to communicate a finished idea, but tools for thinking and "having a conversation" with the problem. [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAEShAEBxCJF16WW0R0IEeJ70DrTWq8xYLTbvmISskXJpX6pMDgdbP3yMgtPCxKRIcWtAaRSjZmDb3WDMbZcnkw1y7QCHk1MXFNnl8RfJRZnUn5OCIR46s5ZygoG90FDtUBr0wgTCbKz5aXJDzrgzdRkjXBqOt0WE4x8oOx5zG9S_koBW_oyt5A), [2](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESngEBxCJF129Z__P08bDrEeAnmE99vYIbKONIAcgNFnP8sgLgmWr128cQ2ctA6Hy8cVRAntV8JMeEfCkUoKoi1Vf-g4vY3UTlFaO9p3S7qT5RZqkUp-IwMA5J3hV8RZ_UhYM_LmPDHtg4LS9ezs8J3piAwhFX-xTWDIfGI9tFyW4vGq4GTkM_385QQCV5OaCynazjM_QqIl2YJ_uhNtgb9w), [3](https://books.google.at/books?id=0woDlFpL5xoC)]

- **Moving:** Making design proposals or "moves" to generate potential solutions. This involves both making primary, foundational leaps (driven by guiding principles) and smaller developmental adjustments. [[1](https://books.google.at/books?id=0woDlFpL5xoC), [2](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESngEBxCJF129Z__P08bDrEeAnmE99vYIbKONIAcgNFnP8sgLgmWr128cQ2ctA6Hy8cVRAntV8JMeEfCkUoKoi1Vf-g4vY3UTlFaO9p3S7qT5RZqkUp-IwMA5J3hV8RZ_UhYM_LmPDHtg4LS9ezs8J3piAwhFX-xTWDIfGI9tFyW4vGq4GTkM_385QQCV5OaCynazjM_QqIl2YJ_uhNtgb9w), [3](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESkgEBxCJF10xU2MpznHk9jv31MqchKnOnqIqz6M7Hias-Lpit5B9_KOmSWA6P86yRUZr26njlYrvZsUhYQH8qkA7RNR3GSoOdG9O6UCBa4s4ELW-NBwreyPGiRm1UTSnmvC5SlVNtQ3HBnWvgZG5rFxzr143oW2HwDJZMSsuvHrT5OB_QUYi6ZyZkqlbD0kNWAHt0MQ)]

- **Bringing Problems and Solutions Together:** Unlike traditional science, where analysis precedes synthesis, designers use solution ideas to better understand the problem. Problem and solution co-evolve. [[1](https://books.google.at/books?id=0woDlFpL5xoC)]

- **Evaluating:** Judging the worth of design moves against both objective criteria (regulations, cost, physics) and subjective value judgments (aesthetics, user experience). Lawson notes that experienced designers know _when_ to suspend judgment to allow creativity and _when_ to apply critical evaluation. [[1](https://books.google.at/books?id=0woDlFpL5xoC), [2](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESkgEBxCJF10xU2MpznHk9jv31MqchKnOnqIqz6M7Hias-Lpit5B9_KOmSWA6P86yRUZr26njlYrvZsUhYQH8qkA7RNR3GSoOdG9O6UCBa4s4ELW-NBwreyPGiRm1UTSnmvC5SlVNtQ3HBnWvgZG5rFxzr143oW2HwDJZMSsuvHrT5OB_QUYi6ZyZkqlbD0kNWAHt0MQ)]

- **Reflecting:** Stepping back from the immediate activity to monitor the overall process. Borrowing from Donald Schön's concept of "reflection-in-action," Lawson describes how designers reflect both _in_ action (evaluating a sketch as it is drawn) and _on_ action (rethinking their broader strategy or direction). [[1](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAEShAEBxCJF16WW0R0IEeJ70DrTWq8xYLTbvmISskXJpX6pMDgdbP3yMgtPCxKRIcWtAaRSjZmDb3WDMbZcnkw1y7QCHk1MXFNnl8RfJRZnUn5OCIR46s5ZygoG90FDtUBr0wgTCbKz5aXJDzrgzdRkjXBqOt0WE4x8oOx5zG9S_koBW_oyt5A), [2](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESbgHEIkXXFVrogHzICxW6u6Yv5D56u1GtltvKbWKLbfzd2BANqV52gosQTo493AM4b7WgJHScO0Q0Bfl_jWCXiYXc-59Fhfg2lESSqL6nTtQmRw2xN4Sh8RqTYB5W9cYjMQz6lHZtE0eaY9TDhcsa), [3](https://books.google.at/books?id=0woDlFpL5xoC)]

3. How the Model Works in Practice

Lawson illustrates these skills not as a flowchart with arrows pointing from start to finish, but as a **constellation of interconnected activities**. A designer may be "moving" and "representing" simultaneously while "evaluating" a partial solution, which in turn forces them to go back to "formulating" the problem differently. [[1](https://books.google.at/books?id=0woDlFpL5xoC), [2](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESngEBxCJF129Z__P08bDrEeAnmE99vYIbKONIAcgNFnP8sgLgmWr128cQ2ctA6Hy8cVRAntV8JMeEfCkUoKoi1Vf-g4vY3UTlFaO9p3S7qT5RZqkUp-IwMA5J3hV8RZ_UhYM_LmPDHtg4LS9ezs8J3piAwhFX-xTWDIfGI9tFyW4vGq4GTkM_385QQCV5OaCynazjM_QqIl2YJ_uhNtgb9w)]

He concludes the chapter by arguing that design education should focus on nurturing these specific cognitive skills—and teaching students how to manage the balance between them—rather than teaching a standardized, step-by-step design method.
