[Designing exploratory design games: a framework for participation in Participatory Design? - Brandt - 2006]
Focuses on exploratory Design Games [p.2]. Categorizes them along aspects of designing [p.3] into 4 kinds of exploratory design games [p.5] The Games Design depends on the intention and the people to be involved [p.5] : Functional categorization (purpose oriented)

_Games to Conceptualize Designing_ [p.3]
Games utilize game pieces on a game board. Those pieces are stylized and abstracted to eliminate functional knowledge and experiences designers already bring with them. Purpose of the games is to be a tool for research. 

_Exchange Perspective Games_ [p.3-4]
Uses the element of chance. Often starts with pieces set in the real world and then builds on change and imagination to combine them in new ways. E.g. using a dice to decide what question to address next [p.10] Purpose is to get out of habits [p.10]

_Negotiation and Work-flow Oriented Games_ [p.4]
Focus on creating a common understanding and language of the work context to design for. Participants usually play themselves. Primary aim is for designers to understand existing work practice. 

_Scenario Oriented Games_ [p.4-5]
Participants enact scenarios using simple props. Focus is often on the functionality of the artifact to be designed. Inspiration from Forum Theater. 

Further categorized in aspects that distinquish powerful exploratory design games from less powerful ones [p.5 onward] :

_Creating Common Images of the Users_ [p.5-7]

_Staging Explorative As-if-worlds_ [p.7-8]
Aim at creating future visions. Games have explicit rules for participation. Assignments are open ended, giving participants the possibility to influence what to focus on in the design. Played in informal atmosphere [p.8]

_Enacting Exploratory Design Games_ [p.8-9]
Using scenarios that are enacted to develop empathy for the users and situations of use; and to design while acting out scenarios in situ [p.8]. Also as a technique for creating, testing and presenting design ideas [p.9]. 

Mentions that one should not be too strict about where which game is applicable [p.9]

[Design games in codesign: as a tool, a mindset and a structure - Vaajakallio - 2014]
Identifies 4 main purposes of employing design games:
- _(1) for research_: To study design actions in a manipulable environment. Player moves are limited by existing rules, conventions and principles. Mentions Habraken and Gross [p.3]
- _(2) for building design competence_: As a way of teaching design students how to establish social interaction in a participatory design process [p.3]
- _(3) for empowering users_: Provide tools for establishing a common language between designers and users, and involve users in discussions. Mentions democratisation of workplaces [p.4]
- _(4) for engaging multiple stakeholders_: Engage diverse stakeholders in expressing, negotiating and generating a shared understanding of users, use contexts and technology in early concept design [p.4]

Mentions that Brandt 2006 categorizes design games by functions in relation to codesign [p.4] But design games often imply more than one of the four functions introduced by brandt, for example negotiation seems to be central to all design games [p.5]

Thus 4 core functions are presented, that exist simultaneously in most design games [p.5] :
- _(1) creating a common design language_
- _(2) promoting a creative and explorative attitude_
- _(3) facilitating the players in envisioning and enacting 'what could be'_
- _(4) helping to define the roles of participants in the interaction during the session_

To guide and support these functions. design games include rules and tangible game pieces, that guide the design move and participation. Rules and forms differ from game to game [p.5]

Main application phase for design games discussed can be described as concept search [p.5]. 

Also develops the "play framework" that offers a simplified categorisation of design games based on 3 different perspectives of experiencing design games [p.7-8] :
- _designers perspective_: Games as _tools_. To organise dialogs, to identify, frame and solve design problems together with users and stakeholders. 
- _players perspective_: Games as _mindset_. Create an experience of being in a game world, a magic circle, with special ordering of time, roles and rules. 
- _design game designers perspective_: Games offer a _structure_. With design game materials that are explicit, while open to reinterpretation, rules and performance roles that can be modified depending on contextual needs. 

Also mentions a categorization of participant roles [p.12-13] :
- _sourcers_: bring personal stories, experiences and knowledge
- _producers_: makes connections between own knowledge and participants insights and game materials
- _performers_: improvise scenarios, dive in into their roles.

[Card-based design tools: a review and analysis of 155 card decks for designers and designing - Roy and Warren - 2019]
Reviews and classifies card-based design tools. Online others (Miemis 2012) categorised 21 card decks into (Design) Principles & Processes; (User) Experience & Game Design; Communication & Learning; Visioning & Foresight; Ideation and Brainstorming [p.7]. Anderson (2012) lists 38 card-based tools categorized into Creative thinking; Design methods; Visual design; Gaming; Management/organisation; Psychology + Design; Social Design and Miscellaneous [p.7]

Wölfel and Merrir (2013) categorized card-based tools based on their purpose, function and characteristics into the broad categories: 
- **General/Repository tools**: Provide inspiration, challenge designers to take another point of view. E.g. Oblique Strategies. Others function as 'methods repositories', e.g. IDEO Method Cards.
- **Participatory design cards**: Develop empathy for the context; engage users and designers in the design process; promote debate. e.g. PictureCARD, Questionable Concepts
- **Context specific/agenda-driven examples**: Cards focused on a specific agenda, e.g. Designing for sustainability or designing for acoustic expression in computer games, e.g. Sound Design Deck. 

In this Paper (Roy and Warren) they analysed 155 card-based design tools and classified them according to the following categories based on each tools main purpose or function and its content [p.10-11] :
- **Systematic design methods and procedures**: Methods and processes to analyse and solve design problems
- **Human-centered design**: Decks of methods to help designers focus on the users, considering their needs, wishes and requirements. 
- **Domain-specific design**: Provide methods, information or checklists for a certain domain. 
- **Creative thinking and problem solving**: Tools for creative thinking
- **Team building and collaborative working**: Aimed to help create effective design teams and facilitate collaborative working
- **Futures thinking**: Awareness of change, planning or problem solving based on trends or analyses of future scenarios

Additionally they identified 3 cross-cutting groups of card decks that overlap the categories mentioned above [p.11] (According to google AI they also overlap with exploratory design games by brandt)
- **aim to facilitate direct end-user participation**: e.g. Layout Kit
- **aim to help designers consider user abilities, needs and wants**: e.g. DSD Cards
- **aim to aid collaboration between different experts or professions**: e.g. DSKD Method Cards

_On how are the card-based design tools supposed to work?_
Möller (2014) groups cards based on what they are supposed to do [p.14] :
- **Framework cards** (task checklists)
- **Index cards** (digests of information or methods)
- **Libraries cards** (useful data)
- **Strategy cards** (thinking strategies)
- **Grid cards** (selection criteria)
- **Visual inspiration cards** (pictorial prompts)

Based on that and with own analysis 5 types and mechanisms are identified [p14-17] :
- **Cards that provide prompts to stimulate creative thinking**: People can be triggered out of their normal thinking patterns by unusal, associations, suggestions or actions written / illustrated on the cards. 
- **Cards that summarise good design practice, know-how or information**: Provide digests of useful information for specific design tasks, e.g. good practice in web design. 
- **Cards that summarise design methods**: Provide summaries for design methods, e.g. Empathy tools from the IDEO Method Cards
- **Cards that provide concepts for specific design problems or domains**: e.g. Design with Intent cards - provide ideas and solutions for influencing human behaviour through design to improve usability among others
- **Cards that provide checklists to aid specific design tasks**:  e.g. Most of MethodKit, with cards each with a key issue to consider while building a website

[Design games as fieldwork: Re-visiting design games from a design anthropological perspective - Kjaersgaard - 2021]
**Just for context**:
Game "Dads round" in the 7th meeting "Parenting roles" [p.5-6]. Consultants found this meeting particulary problematic and in need of tools to facilitate dialogues and enhance the notion of empathy - the fathers ability to see things from the childrens perspective. 

Exploratory Design Game: Doll scenarios use Scenario playing. Provided also insights into consultants world - insider language, how they understand father groups, made visible their agendas in dicussion and different approaches. [p.9] Value of this design game was thus the ability to serve as fieldwork to provide insight into the perspectives present in consulting, and challenge implicit design assumptions about family therapy [p.10]. 

Story card Game: Children tell stories based on 4 picture comic strip they draw or retell existing ones from their perspective [p.10] then played "Go Fish" with the story cards which created lively discussions [p.11]. Family consultants rejected most of the stories because they were aiming for constructive stories that focus more on the inmate as father than as inmate [p.12]. When approaching this design game as a form of interventionist fieldwork, it could also be used to shed light on consultants perspective and their concepts [p.13]. As such they serve as a tool for exploring, challenging and changing perspectives of both researchers and participants [p.14]. 

Rehearsing the future - workshop was conducted with consultants taking roles as game masters and fathers to familiarize themselves with the game. By doing so it also showed the researches a glimpse of current roles, practices and perspectives of the consultants. [p.17-18]

[Technology Cards: A Design Game for Navigating in a  Future of Digital Technologies - Nymark - 2023]
Mentions design games developed by purpose such as [p.2]
- investigating a design problem
- influencing user behavior
- facilitate collaboration
- generate ideas

Highlights that design games fall within the category of design tools which are instruments of enquiry and posses one or more of the following five basic qualitites: [p.3]
- _Perception_: Revealing otherwise hidden facets of a design situation
- _Conception_: Helping designers understand the problem and examine possible solutions
- _Externalization_: Evaluate imagined design solutions as part of the world
- _Knowing-through-action_: Generate knowledge through acting with an instrument
- _Mediation_: Exchange insights between actors and artefacts and coordinate actions

Of reviewed design tools 72% were or included card decks [p.3]. 
They mention technology-oriented design games [p.3]. They then focus on a _branch of design games_ - design games to aid futures thinking [p.3] (building on Roy and Warren 2019 - _futures thinking_) - and further divide it into 3 groups:
- Games that focus on _idea generation and conceptualisation_
- Games that focus on _promoting better designs or change_
- Games that focus on _anticipating the impact of trends and technologies_
Technology cards also fall within that thrid group [p.3-4]. 

They conducted sessions involving their technology cards as [p.9]
- Game 1 - Which technologies do we find most important? (Order the cards by importance as a group)
- Game 2 - Select a combination of 2-4 technologies that represent an idea for digitalisation
- Game 3 - How does the future look like and how do we get there? (common vision of future by combining cards - then discuss which steps are necessary to reach that vision)

The presented Technology cards Game posesses 4 of the qualitites mentioned above [p.11] 
- _Perception_: Participants identified design challenges by presenting examples of current challenges to justify their viewpoints when priotising technologies [p.11]
- _Conception_: By helping people examine and obtain an overview of technologies that could improve their business [p.12]
- _knowing-through-action_: Participants clustered technologies that they considered to be related or interdependent [p.12]
- _mediating_: The game like format of the cards encouraged participants to take turns joining the discussion

[Staging situated negotiation games for (re)designing local healthcare facilities and services - Pedersen - 2025]
Describes policy games as a subset of serious games [p.4]. Design Games are described as tools for collaboratively exploring problems and solutions iteratively, while policy games are played during specific events and in stand-alone workshop settings [p4-5].

Negotation is further described as a central part to both design and policy games. Not surprising, since negotiation is a core aspect of collaborative design [p.5]. Also plays a central role in the actor-network theory [p.6]. 

Describes thus a Negotiation Games Framework "Staging Negotation Spaces" based on a theatrical metapher and combining theory from ANT, methods from participatory design such as games. [p.6-7]. 

Developed three types of negotiation games: [p.9]
- _Configuration games_: For mapping and reflection of current practices and roles. Learn about current situation. Serve as baseline for discussing configuration of future scenarios. 
- _Prioritisation games_: Ranking aspects or problems. Keywords as game pieces, flexibility in rules
- _Process oriented games_: Negotiating and configuring care pathways. Focus on iterative problem solving.

They all share: Game boards, pieces and question cards as structuring elements. [p.8] 
Other elements of games, like turn-taking or change were absent [p.24]. The negotiation games served as boundary objects [p.24].
