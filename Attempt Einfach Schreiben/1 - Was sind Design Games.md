[Designing Exploratory Design Games: A Framework for  Participation in Participatory Design? - E.Brandt - 2006]
A way of organizing participation in design projects [p.1]. Participatory design implies active involvement of the people designed for as well as stakeholders. [p.1]. They are frameworks within which participation, dialogue and negotiation takes place. [p.4] Many have used the game metaphor as a way of organizing participation [p.1]. Describes Games as a play with props following specific rules, often with an element of competition between players and decided by chance, strength and/or skill. [p.1] In Design Games participants seldom compete to win, the aim is to jointly explore various possibilities within a game setting [p.2]. Depending on the Game they aim to spark dialogue [p.3], help to create a common understanding of / common language for the context to design for [p.3], explore various design ideas [p.4]. The purpose of participation in a Design Game varies and can be staged in many ways. [p.4] There is no one size fits all Design Game but it is rather encouraged to design the Game specifically for the task [p.4]. Seeing collaboration as Games suggests certain ingredients to how to stage participation [p.4]. For example: Rules, like turn-taking and Game-pieces to make moves seems to make everyone participate on more equal terms [p.6]. Open-end assignments give participants possibility to interpret and influence what to focus on in the design [p.7]. It is also seen as important that participants engage in an informal atmosphere [p.7]. Game-Pieces are used as "things-to-think" and function as boundary-objects [p.7]. 

[Designing educational software with students through collaborative design games: The We!Design&Play framework - G.Triantafyllakos - 2011]
Design Games could provide a starting point for facilitating user participation and contribution [p.2]. Have been proposed in many studies as effective means for idea generation, creativity, improving communication and demoting power relations inside a group. Through the use of familiar game conventions they promote a non-threatening, playful and creative atmosphere [p.2]. Also Games play an important role in young peoples live outside school [p.2]. Design Games share most of attitudinal and motivational aspects of educational games [p.2]. Design Games cultivate a sense of playful exploration among participants [p.2]. They provide a framework for social interaction and establish a relatively egalitarian design environment that demotes power relations [p.2]. Explicit rules provide a driving force throughout the design process and make participants feel more at ease with the role of the designer [p.2]. Design artefacts and props are used as stimuli and provide participants with a shared language, spure their imagination and guide their discussion, while remaining open to different interpretations [p.3]. The playful setting of the Game relaxed participants and undermined the formality of the process [p.14] Game rules reduced the effects such as social loafing, evaluation apprehension and production blocking [p.14]. It remains a responsibility of the facilitator to establish a balance between non-productive nature of the game-like activity and the productive nature of a design process [p.14]

[Design games in codesign: as a tool, a mindset and a structure - Kirsikka Vaajakallio - 2014]
Design Games are a popular concept to describe activities and methods that support collaborative explorations in inspiring atmospheres by engaging users and other partners in collaborative design [p.2]. They are a framework and a tool [p.2]. As a concept they have taken many forms [p.2] The discussed games span three time frames: now, near future and speculative future [p.2]. Design Games provide a stage and tools for people to share experiences in order to envision future ones [p.2-3]. They are tools that emphasise play-qualities, supported by tangible game materials and rules. They highlight the exploratory, imaginative, dialogical and empathic aspects of codesign. The objective of the games is rooted in the design context. The means for reaching the objective are drawn from design practice and the world of games. [p.3]. They are not clearly defined, instead there are several description of their characteristics dependent on the games context. [p.3.] Most description agree that design games are about staging participation, there is seldom competition to win and there are rules and tangible game pieces that guide the design moves [p.3]. Purposes for which design games are applied are "for research", "for building design competence", "for empowering users" and "for engaging multiple stakeholders" [p.4]. Negotiation seems to be central to all design games, especially in codesign. [p.5] For a designer, design games are tools, for players they appear as mindset, for a game designer they offer a structure [p.8]. They contain of two separate but intertwined parts: design and games [p.8]. They are diverse in their purposes, appearances and outcomes [p.13-14] 

[Card-based design tools: a review and analysis of 155 card decks for designers and designing - Robin Roy - 2019]
Examines card-based design tools [p.1]. Early card examples are often bought as a plaything [p.2]. Pictures on the cards can help to stimulate innovative thinking [p.2] They may also be used to widen the search space (for ideas) and overcome mental blocks [p.2]. Tools like card sorting help identify user's mental models of a digital systems information structure. [p.5]. Cards are not prescriptive, rather act as support for inspiration, organisation and communication of ideas [p.6]. They also provide a common basis for understanding, convenient summaries of information and are semi-structured tools between blank post-its and detailed instructions [p.7]. They are a large number of tools available and they are difficult to classify [p.7]. Some can be classified by their main purpose into creative thinking and problem solving, systematic design methods and procedures, human centered design, futures thinking, domain specific methods and team building [p.13]. Their supposed working can be classified into stimulating creative thinking, summarizing good design practice or other information, provide concepts for specific design problems and provide checklists to aid specific design tasks [p.16-17]. Often tools have been tested by the authors, so information is not fully objective [p.19] Very few have been comparing tools with each other or other methods[p.19]. Some tests report that the cards simplified brainstorming, broke the ice, sparked creativity and lowered the barrier for proposing ideas [p.19] They also helped to frame and reframe the problem [p.20]. Card-based tools have advantages over other media, they are engaging objects which summarize information, can be interacted upon in a physical way [p.25]. Good instruction, facilitation and practice in using cards seems to be essential [p.27]. 

[Design games as fieldwork: Re-visiting design games from a design anthropological perspective - Kjaersgaard - 2021]
They see design games as a form of inquiry into the relations, practices and perspectives at the intersection between design and use [p.2]. They do not only have the world of 'users' as their object but also the practices and contexts of design [p.4]. Design Games in Literature have been conceived of as playful material for letting users and stakeholders participate as co-designers in early stages of the design process [p.3]. They enable designers to learn about users and contexts of use, provide a shared language to facilitate the meeting and mutual learning between the people involved [p.3]. They also have the potential to level out power relations between users and stakeholders [p.3]. It possible to use them (story-card-game) to facilitate a workshop with therapeutic, entertaining and design research activity [p.10.] Using design games as tools to facilitate collaboration and early concept development, we might overlook their potentials for prompting insights into situated practices and perspectives at the intersection between design and everyday life [p.19.]. By exploring "what could be", we simultaneously understood more about "what is" [p.19.]. When rules changed during game, the participants priorities, practices and perspectives stood out much clearer to the researches and the participants themselves [p.19]. Design Games might thus also be understood as a form of design anthropological fieldwork generating insights into the practices and perspectives of others and challenging taken for granted design practices [p.19].
